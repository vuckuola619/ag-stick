**NARRATOR:**  
Until August 14th, 2019. 

That was the morning WeWork filed its official prospectus with the Securities and Exchange Commission. It was supposed to be the triumphant coronation of Silicon Valley's newest titan. 

Instead, it triggered the most spectacular corporate collapse since Enron. 

Within six weeks, the valuation had dropped by forty billion dollars. The CEO was forced out. The IPO was canceled. And the world's most aggressive venture capital fund was left holding a multi-billion-dollar disaster that they had paid top dollar to create.

The question isn't how WeWork failed. Real estate companies fail all the time. 

The question is how an entrepreneur convinced the smartest financiers on Earth that renting drywall was an immortal technology monopoly.

### [01:15 - 04:15] ACT 1: THE DREAM (THE BABYSITTER & THE BEER TAP)

**[VISUAL CUE]**  
*Black-and-white archival photo collage of lower Manhattan, circa 2008. Crumbling Wall Street banks, Lehman Brothers cardboard boxes on the sidewalk. Out of the rubble rises a colorful, hipster paper puppet of Adam Neumann.*

**[SFX]**  
*Muted distant street noise, low double-bass jazz groove kicks in.*

**NARRATOR:**  
To understand how forty-seven billion dollars vanishes, you have to meet Adam Neumann. 

Adam was six-foot-five, possessed shoulder-length rock-star hair, walked around Manhattan meetings completely barefoot, and had an uncanny ability to stare directly into your eyes and convince you that whatever he was selling was destined to change human civilization. 

His early business ventures, however, were not changing civilization. 

His first idea was baby clothing with built-in knee pads to make crawling more comfortable, marketed under the brand "Krawlers." It failed. His second idea was collapsible high-heeled women's shoes. That also failed. 

**[VISUAL CUE]**  
*A baby romper with knee pads drops onto the desk, followed by a cardboard floor plan of 79 Greenpoint Avenue in Brooklyn. Split-screen diagram shows GreenDesk.*

**NARRATOR:**  
Then, in 2008, right as the global financial crisis was crushing New York real estate, Adam and an architectural designer named Miguel McKelvey noticed something in Brooklyn. 

Landlords were sitting on empty warehouse space they couldn't fill. Meanwhile, thousands of newly laid-off bankers, designers, and programmers were trying to work from crowded apartments. 

Adam and Miguel took a single floor in Greenpoint, Brooklyn, threw up some inexpensive glass and plywood partitions, put down a couch, bought an espresso machine, and called it GreenDesk. 

The business model was deceptively simple: lease empty office space from landlords wholesale at a cheap rate, cut it up into tiny glass boxes, and sublease individual desks retail to freelancers at a premium markup. 

**[VISUAL CUE]**  
*A simple paper ruler appears. A big square room labeled "WHOLESALE: $30/sq ft" gets divided with dotted scissors into six tiny boxes labeled "RETAIL: $75/sq ft". Money counter ticks upward.*

**[SFX]**  
*Scissors snipping paper, cash register ding.*

**NARRATOR:**  
It worked immediately. It was profitable, reliable, and straightforward. 

In fact, it was so straightforward that Adam and Miguel sold their share of GreenDesk for a few million dollars and founded WeWork in 2010. 

Their first location opened on Grand Street in SoHo. And this is where Adam made his first crucial insight. 

If you walk into a traditional commercial real estate office in Midtown Manhattan, it feels like an insurance firm. Beige carpet, fluorescent lighting, acoustic ceiling tiles, and quiet despair. 

Adam turned the office into an adult playground. 

**[VISUAL CUE]**  
*Pop-up paper puppet scene: Exposed brick walls, reclaimed wood coffee bar, string lights, a stainless steel kegerator dispensing cold beer, and neon cursive signs reading "DO WHAT YOU LOVE" and "HUSTLE HARDER".*

**NARRATOR:**  
He added exposed brick, Edison light bulbs, polished concrete, infused fruit water, and free draft beer on tap. You weren't a tenant renting seventy square feet of cubicle space; you were a "member" of a revolutionary global tribe. 

If you were a 23-year-old software developer working alone, WeWork gave you the illusion that you worked at Google. 

And for that illusion, people gladly paid three times the underlying rent. 

Within four years, WeWork had expanded across New York, San Francisco, and London. But Adam didn't want to run a successful real estate company. 

Because real estate companies trade at boring valuations. 

**[VISUAL CUE]**  
*A Wall Street financial comparison card drops onto the corkboard. Left side: "REAL ESTATE LANDLORD" with a boring gray 1x revenue multiple. Right side: "TECH PLATFORM" glowing in neon blue with a 25x revenue multiple.*

**NARRATOR:**  
In the stock market, if you are a commercial landlord, investors value your company based on net rental yields. You might trade at twelve to fifteen times earnings, or roughly one to two times revenue. 

But if you are a technology company—if you are Facebook or Salesforce—investors value you at twenty or thirty times revenue, because software can be copied to a billion users overnight at practically zero cost. 

So Adam simply stopped using real estate words. 

He didn't lease offices. He provided "Space-as-a-Service." 

WeWork wasn't a collection of desks; it was a "physical social network." The buildings weren't concrete; they were nodes on a global platform designed to, quote, "elevate the world’s consciousness." 

And in Silicon Valley, where venture capitalists were drowning in zero-interest-rate money, that was all they needed to hear.

### [04:15 - 08:00] ACT 2: THE MACHINE (THE 12-MINUTE VISIT)

**[VISUAL CUE]**  
*Tokyo skyline, SoftBank headquarters. Puppet of Masayoshi Son appears, wearing his signature patterned vest, surrounded by a mountain of hundred-dollar bills labeled "$100 BILLION VISION FUND".*

**[SFX]**  
*Deep electronic hum, tension drums.*

**NARRATOR:**  
By 2016, WeWork had raised hundreds of millions from blue-chip firms like Benchmark Capital and Fidelity. The company was valued at sixteen billion dollars. 

By any traditional financial standard, that was already detached from reality. Regus—an office-sublease company founded in 1989 that had four times as many locations and was actually profitable—was valued at less than three billion. 

WeWork was burning hundreds of millions of dollars a year, yet was worth five times more. 

And then, Masayoshi Son entered the building. 

**[VISUAL CUE]**  
*A vintage black Town Car pulls up to WeWork headquarters in New York. A digital stopwatch reads "12:00" and begins counting down rapidly.*

**NARRATOR:**  
Masayoshi Son was the founder and chief executive of SoftBank. He had made arguably the greatest venture investment in human history when he put twenty million dollars into Alibaba in 2000, turning it into over sixty billion. 

In late 2016, Masa launched the Vision Fund: a staggering one-hundred-billion-dollar pool of capital backed by the sovereign wealth funds of Saudi Arabia and Abu Dhabi. 

Masa was looking for founders who could conquer entire industries. 

In December 2016, Masa arrived at WeWork’s headquarters in Manhattan. He was over two hours late. He walked in, looked at Adam, and announced that he only had twelve minutes. 

Adam gave him a lightning-fast tour through the bustling common spaces, the beer taps, the neon signs, and the communal tables. 

When the twelve minutes were up, Masa invited Adam to ride with him in his car. In the backseat, Masa pulled out an iPad, sketched out the outline of an investment, and asked Adam a question: 

**[VISUAL CUE]**  
*Split screen: Masa Son puppet on the left, Adam Neumann puppet on the right. Dialogue balloons pop up like an editorial comic.*

**NARRATOR:**  
"In a fight, who wins? The smart guy, or the crazy guy?" 

Adam replied: "The crazy guy." 

Masa nodded. "You are right. But you are not crazy enough." 

Masa told Adam that his current growth targets were far too small. He told him to make WeWork ten times bigger. He told him that in a war, being right doesn't matter as much as being enormous. 

Over the next two years, SoftBank poured more than ten billion dollars directly into WeWork. 

**[VISUAL CUE]**  
*A global map unrolls. Pushpins slam into cities across the world: London, Shanghai, Tokyo, Paris, São Paulo, Berlin. A calendar shows dates flipping like a hurricane.*

**[SFX]**  
*Rapid stapler clicks, cash swoosh.*

**NARRATOR:**  
With SoftBank’s firehose behind him, Adam abandoned all financial restraint. 

WeWork began opening an entire floor of new office space somewhere on Earth every single day. They leased iconic skyscrapers in London, whole blocks in Manhattan, prime towers in Tokyo. 

If a landlord asked for forty dollars a square foot, WeWork offered fifty just to lock out competitors. 

They bought an elementary school called WeGrow, designed by Bjarke Ingels, where children took lessons in entrepreneurship and mindfulness. They launched WeLive, where twenty-somethings lived in furnished micro-apartments. 

They even bought a forty-two-million-dollar stake in a Spanish company that manufactured artificial wave pools for surfing. 

Why? Because Adam loved to surf. 

**[VISUAL CUE]**  
*Adam Neumann puppet surfing on an artificial wave made of stacked twenty-dollar bills, while office desks float in the background.*

**NARRATOR:**  
To anyone watching the headlines, WeWork looked invincible. By January 2019, SoftBank finalized another investment that valued WeWork at forty-seven billion dollars. 

Adam told employees that WeWork would one day be worth a trillion dollars. He told associates he wanted to be the world's first trillionaire, live forever, and become President of the World. 

Inside the company's finance department, however, an entirely different reality was unfolding. 

Because WeWork had built its entire empire on a foundational economic flaw known as the Asset-Liability Duration Mismatch. 

**[VISUAL CUE]**  
*Motion Graphic Diagram: An anchor attached to a heavy steel chain labeled "15-YEAR LEASE OBLIGATION ($47.2B)" dropping down to the sea floor. Suspended above the water is a tiny paper umbrella labeled "30-DAY MEMBER CANCELATION ($4.0B)".*

**NARRATOR:**  
Here is how the trap works. 

When WeWork opened a location, it signed a legally binding, long-term commercial lease with a landlord. Those leases typically lasted ten to fifteen years. Rain or shine, recession or boom, WeWork owed that landlord fixed rent every single month. 

On the other side of the equation, how long were WeWork's customers committed to paying? 

Often, thirty days. 

Freelancers and small startups could cancel their memberships on a month's notice whenever money got tight. 

It was the financial equivalent of taking out a thirty-year fixed mortgage on a house and trying to pay it off by subleasing the guest room on Airbnb for weekend parties. If tourists stop showing up, you still owe the bank. 

By mid-2019, WeWork had signed forty-seven point two billion dollars in future lease commitments to landlords. 

Its committed revenue from customers? 

Barely four billion. 

**[VISUAL CUE]**  
*A bar chart comparing $47.2B (towering skyscraper) vs $4.0B (tiny wooden shed).*

**NARRATOR:**  
To hide this problem from investors, WeWork's executives did what any visionary tech company does when the numbers don't work. 

They invented their own math. 

In their financial reports, WeWork introduced a metric they called "Community Adjusted EBITDA." 

**[VISUAL CUE]**  
*A physical accounting sheet drops onto the desk. A red magnifying glass zooms in on the line item: "COMMUNITY ADJUSTED EBITDA".*

**NARRATOR:**  
Traditional EBITDA stands for Earnings Before Interest, Taxes, Depreciation, and Amortization. It is already a non-standard metric that companies use to make their operations look cleaner. 

WeWork took EBITDA, and then removed general and administrative expenses. Then they removed marketing expenses. And then—in a stroke of accounting genius—they removed building-level operating costs. 

Which included the rent. 

They were an office subleasing business whose primary operational expense was rent. And by calculating profitability without including the rent, they announced to the world that their offices were wildly profitable. 

As Bloomberg columnist Matt Levine dryly noted: if you simply ignore the costs of running your business, almost every business makes money.

### [08:00 - 11:30] ACT 3: THE CRACK (THE S-1 CARNIVAL)

**[VISUAL CUE]**  
*A heavy stack of printed legal paper drops onto the wooden desk with a massive thud. Title: "UNITED STATES SECURITIES AND EXCHANGE COMMISSION // FORM S-1 // THE WE COMPANY".*

**[SFX]**  
*Heavy paper thud, silence, then a low cello suspense line.*

**NARRATOR:**  
By the summer of 2019, the music was about to stop. 

The SoftBank Vision Fund was running low on cash. Saudi Arabia and Abu Dhabi were refusing to let Masa Son pour another sixteen billion dollars into WeWork. 

If WeWork wanted to keep fueling its billion-dollar burn rate, it had to do what every private unicorn eventually must: go public on Wall Street. 

On August 14th, 2019, WeWork publicly filed its Form S-1 with the SEC. 

For nine years, Adam Neumann had controlled the narrative through private meetings, confidential pitch decks, and magnetic charm. 

The S-1 stripped all of that away. Under federal law, you cannot charm the SEC. You have to tell the truth. 

And the truth was astonishing. 

**[VISUAL CUE]**  
*The dedication page of the S-1 unfolds. Close-up on the text: "We dedicate this to the energy of we - greater than any one of us but inside each of us." A handwritten red sticky note appears next to it: "Seriously?"*

**NARRATOR:**  
The document opened with a dedication: 

"We dedicate this to the energy of we—greater than any one of us but inside each of us." 

When analysts turned past the spiritual poetry to the consolidated financial statements on page F-4, they found horror. 

In 2018, WeWork had generated one point eight billion dollars in revenue. 

Its net loss was one point nine billion dollars. 

The company was losing more than a dollar for every dollar of revenue it brought through the door. Over the preceding three years, it had incinerated over four billion dollars of cash. 

**[VISUAL CUE]**  
*Forensic document reveal. Yellow highlighter marks the "Related Party Transactions" section of the S-1.*

**NARRATOR:**  
Worse than the losses, however, was the brazen self-dealing disclosed in the fine print. 

Buried inside the related-party transactions section, investors discovered that Adam Neumann had been operating WeWork not as a public stewardship, but as his own private piggy bank. 

Item one: Adam owned personal financial stakes in commercial real estate buildings in New York and Chicago. He then leased those buildings back to WeWork as the tenant, collecting millions of dollars in personal rent from the very company he was running. 

**[VISUAL CUE]**  
*Puppet animation: Adam Neumann puppet sitting at a desk, handing a giant check from his right hand to his left hand.*

**NARRATOR:**  
Item two: In early 2019, WeWork decided to rebrand as "The We Company." 

Adam had previously trademarked the word "We" through a private entity he controlled called WE Holdings LLC. 

So Adam’s company paid Adam's private entity five point nine million dollars in stock to purchase the rights to the word "We." 

He sold a common English pronoun back to his own company for six million dollars. 

**[VISUAL CUE]**  
*A dictionary card for the word "WE". A price tag of "$5,900,000" is slapped onto the definition.*

**NARRATOR:**  
Item three: WeWork had spent over sixty million dollars to buy a brand-new Gulfstream G650 private jet, which Adam and his family used to fly around the globe, frequently leaving trails of tequila glasses behind. 

Item four: Corporate governance did not exist. 

Adam held Class B and Class C super-voting shares that gave him twenty votes per share, giving him absolute monarchical control over the company. 

And if Adam died or became permanently incapacitated? 

The S-1 revealed that his successor would not be chosen by the board of directors. The successor would be chosen by a three-person committee—which included Adam’s wife, Rebekah Neumann. 

**[VISUAL CUE]**  
*A mock royal family tree card on textured paper. Adam at the top wearing a paper crown, Rebekah holding a gavel, and the board of directors drawn as tiny mice tied with red string.*

**NARRATOR:**  
Wall Street looked at the S-1 and didn't see a tech revolution. 

They saw a commercial real estate company bleeding cash, disguised as a spiritual cult, governed like an absolute monarchy. 

The reaction was immediate, visceral, and unanimous. 

NYU business professor Scott Galloway published an essay titled "WeWTF," laying out the math in stark terms: WeWork was an unsustainable pyramid of optimism whose business model was fundamentally broken. 

The tech press, Wall Street analysts, and institutional fund managers joined the chorus. 

Within days, the IPO roadshow hadn't even started, and the forty-seven-billion-dollar valuation was already dead.

### [11:30 - 14:30] ACT 4: THE COLLISION (THE 33-DAY COLLAPSE)

**[VISUAL CUE]**  
*A dramatic timeline board. Cards flip over in rapid succession: AUGUST 14 → SEPTEMBER 04 → SEPTEMBER 13 → SEPTEMBER 17 → SEPTEMBER 24.*

**[SFX]**  
*Rapid typewriter clatter, dramatic orchestral strings building.*

**NARRATOR:**  
What followed over the next thirty-three days was one of the fastest unravelings in business history. 

WeWork’s investment bankers at JPMorgan Chase and Goldman Sachs had originally whispered to Adam that WeWork could go public at a valuation of sixty-five billion dollars, or perhaps even one hundred billion. 

By early September, as institutional investors refused to touch the stock, the bankers slashed the proposed IPO valuation to twenty billion dollars. 

Investors still balked. 

They slashed it to fifteen billion. Then twelve billion. Then ten billion. 

At ten billion dollars, WeWork was worth less than the total capital it had raised. 

Adam began making frantic concessions. 

**[VISUAL CUE]**  
*An amendment filing Form S-1/A drops onto the desk. Yellow marker strikes out "20 votes" and writes "10 votes". Another strike removes Rebekah from the succession clause. A check for $5,900,000 is stamped "RETURNED".*

**NARRATOR:**  
On September 13th, WeWork filed an amendment to its prospectus. 

Adam agreed to cut his super-voting rights from twenty votes to ten votes per share. 

Rebekah was removed from the succession committee. 

And Adam announced that he would return the five point nine million dollars for the "We" trademark back to the company. 

It didn't matter. The dam had already broken. 

And then came the fatal blow. 

**[VISUAL CUE]**  
*A bank contract titled "$6,000,000,000 CONTINGENT CREDIT FACILITY - JPMORGAN & GOLDMAN SACHS". A giant red padlock snaps shut on top of the document.*

**[SFX]**  
*Heavy metallic lock snap.*

**NARRATOR:**  
To fund its operations through 2020, WeWork had negotiated a massive six-billion-dollar debt package with JPMorgan and a consortium of banks. 

But there was a catch. 

The entire six billion dollars in debt was legally contingent on WeWork raising at least three billion dollars in equity from the IPO. 

If there was no IPO, there was no six-billion-dollar loan. 

And without that nine billion dollars of fresh cash, WeWork’s internal spreadsheets revealed the arithmetic of doom: 

With a burn rate exceeding two hundred million dollars a month, WeWork would run out of money before Thanksgiving. 

The company was thirty days from missing payroll and defaulting on leases across dozens of countries. 

**[VISUAL CUE]**  
*A split-screen emergency board meeting. Puppet of Masayoshi Son on a Tokyo video call, puppets of Benchmark Capital's Bruce Dunlevie and SoftBank executives staring down Adam Neumann.*

**NARRATOR:**  
On September 17th, WeWork officially postponed the IPO. 

Behind closed doors, WeWork’s board of directors—led by SoftBank and Benchmark Capital—realized there was only one move left to save the company from immediate Chapter 11 bankruptcy. 

Adam had to go. 

On September 24th, 2019, after an emergency board meeting, Adam Neumann stepped down as Chief Executive Officer. 

In thirty-three days, the visionary who was going to live forever and lead a forty-seven-billion-dollar empire had been stripped of his company.

### [14:30 - 16:30] ACT 5: THE AFTERMATH (THE GOLDEN PARACHUTE)

**[VISUAL CUE]**  
*SoftBank logo with a lifesaver ring thrown across the screen. A rescue contract drops down.*

**[SFX]**  
*Subtle, melancholic piano notes.*

**NARRATOR:**  
With Adam out, WeWork was still bleeding millions of dollars every single day. 

To prevent the company from collapsing into immediate liquidation, SoftBank was forced to step in with a humiliating nine-point-five billion dollar rescue package in October 2019. 

SoftBank took full control of the wreckage at an implied valuation of less than eight billion dollars. 

Thousands of employees were laid off. Workers who had worked seventy-hour weeks on the promise that their stock options would make them millionaires discovered their equity was worth zero. 

**[VISUAL CUE]**  
*A paper trash bin overflowing with stock option certificates labeled "WORTHLESS". Next to it, Adam Neumann puppet relaxing on a luxury yacht holding an enormous sack of cash labeled "$480 MILLION".*

**NARRATOR:**  
And what happened to Adam Neumann? 

Did he lose everything? 

Not quite. 

As part of the initial rescue package, SoftBank agreed to pay Adam a one-hundred-and-eighty-five million dollar consulting fee, refinance a five-hundred-million-dollar personal credit line, and offer to buy hundreds of millions of dollars of his personal shares. 

When SoftBank later tried to back out of purchasing his shares during the COVID crash, Adam sued SoftBank in the Delaware Court of Chancery. 

In February 2021, they settled. 

Adam walked away with roughly four hundred and eighty million dollars in cash from stock sales, another fifty million for his legal bills, and fifty million in non-compete payments. 

Adam Neumann had presided over the destruction of forty billion dollars in market value, and walked away half a billion dollars richer. 

**[VISUAL CUE]**  
*Newspaper clippings from November 2023. Headline: "WEWORK FILES FOR CHAPTER 11 BANKRUPTCY". A gavel slams down.*

**[SFX]**  
*Wood gavel strike.*

**NARRATOR:**  
Two years later, WeWork finally stumbled onto the public markets through a SPAC merger in late 2021. 

Without the hype, the market treated WeWork for what it had always been: an unprofitable landlord. 

The stock plummeted ninety-nine percent. 

On November 6th, 2023, WeWork officially filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy in New Jersey federal court, listing eighteen billion dollars in debt. 

Its original common shareholders, SoftBank’s equity, and employee options were completely wiped out.

### [16:30 - 17:00] FINAL PAYOFF & BRIDGE

**[VISUAL CUE]**  
*The corkboard camera pulls back to a wide shot. The desk is cleared except for a simple wooden office chair and a coffee cup under a single desk lamp.*

**NARRATOR:**  
The lesson of WeWork was never really about Adam Neumann’s charisma, or the free beer, or the spiritual nonsense in the SEC filings. 

The lesson was about the financial incentive of venture capital. 

Silicon Valley’s business model depends on discovering things that scale infinitely with software. But an office is not software. 

A desk is a physical object made of wood and metal. A building is concrete and glass. You cannot copy and paste real estate. 

When you pour billions of dollars of tech capital into an ancient, low-margin business, you don't change the laws of physics. 

You just create a very expensive illusion. 

**[VISUAL CUE]**  
*A paper card slides into frame from the right side. It depicts a prototype semi-truck resting on top of a desert hill in Utah. A yellow arrow points down the hill.*

**NARRATOR:**  
And WeWork wasn't the only company that learned you cannot bluff the laws of physics with visionary optimism. 

Because just as WeWork was collapsing in New York, another billionaire was convincing Wall Street that he had built the world's most advanced zero-emissions hydrogen truck. 

Except there was one tiny detail. 

The truck didn't have an engine.
